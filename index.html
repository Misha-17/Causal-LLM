CLadder · Causal reasoning in language models

How do LLMs perform
on causal reasoning?

What the questions ask, how the answers are calculated, and where the evaluated models still make mistakes.

01

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are the AI systems that generate responses in chat assistants. When we ask one whether a decision will help, we are asking it to do more than spot a pattern. Imagine that people who use an AI assistant finish more tasks. Does the assistant cause that improvement, or do its users already have easier tasks? And if someone failed a task without the assistant, would using it have changed the outcome? These questions sound similar, but they need different kinds of reasoning. Distinguishing them matters whenever a model helps us decide what to do.

The difficulty is that a convincing explanation can still use the wrong causal rule. A model might confuse “people who chose an action” with “people made to take that action,” or calculate the right number for the wrong question. CLadder, introduced by Jin and colleagues, makes these mistakes testable: it creates small, controlled causal problems with answers that can be calculated. However, one overall score can hide weaknesses, and success on a fixed public set of questions does not tell us whether the same ranking will hold on newly generated examples.

Here, we compare nine reported model configurations on CLadder v1.5 and then examine GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 on a separately generated set of CLadder-style questions. We explain what each task asks, show accuracy for each kind of reasoning where available, and look more closely at individual question types. The comparison also records the available reasoning settings, because a model given extra computation is not being tested under the same conditions as one answering without it.

The main result is that high overall accuracy does not mean equally strong performance everywhere. GPT-5.5 leads the fixed-set comparison at 95.09%. On the generated batch, it scores 92.87%, compared with 90.00% for Claude Opus 4.8. Their gap is only 0.40 percentage points on association questions, but 5.44 points on counterfactual questions. Both reach 100% on the batch’s backdoor-adjustment questions, yet score only 54.21% and 58.11% on collider-bias questions. The useful question is therefore not just which model scores highest, but which causal operations it can apply reliably.

02 · The benchmark, explained

What is CLadder, and how does it work?

CLadder is a collection of short causal-reasoning questions with known answers. Each question describes a small world, gives the relevant facts, and asks for a “yes” or “no.”

The world is generated from mathematical rules. Its variables are usually binary: for example, an assistant is used or not used, and a task is completed or not completed. Because the generator knows the rules, a causal solver can derive a reference answer. The language model must reach that answer from the question’s text.

What does “data” mean here?

Usually, it means probabilities stated inside the question, such as “70% of people in this group complete the task.” CLadder does not normally hand the model a spreadsheet and ask it to discover what causes what. It tests whether the model can reason from a described causal structure and the information supplied.

Three kinds of question

CLadder covers the three levels of Pearl’s ladder of causation. Keep the same example in mind: X means using an assistant and Y means completing a task. The value 1 means “yes”; 0 means “no.”

Rung 1 · Seeing

Association

Among people who used the assistant, how many completed the task? We are describing an observed group.

P(Y = 1 | X = 1)
Rung 2 · Doing

Intervention

What would happen if we made people use the assistant? We are changing the action they take.

P(Y = 1 | do(X = 1))
Rung 3 · Imagining

Counterfactual

For someone who did not use it and failed, how likely is it that they would have succeeded if they had used it?

P(Y1 = 1 | X = 0, Y = 0)

Read P as “probability” and the vertical bar | as “given.” The expression do(X = 1) means we actively set X to 1. The subscript in Y1 means the outcome under that action. These levels need different information; a higher rung is not a guarantee that every question will be harder for a model.

The mathematical model behind the questions

A structural causal model, or SCM, is a set of rules describing how variables are produced. “Structural” means the rules represent cause and effect, not just correlations.

𝓜 = (U, V, F, P(U)),   Vj = fj(Paj, Uj)Each variable is determined by its direct causes and background factors.
V
The variables in the model, such as assistant use and task completion.
U
Background factors, such as prior experience, that this model does not explain further.
F
The collection of rules f that produce the variables.
Paj
The “parents” of variable j: its direct causes in the graph.
P(U)
How likely the different background conditions are.

An intervention replaces the rule for X with the instruction “set X to x.” In a causal graph, this removes arrows pointing into X. Other mechanisms stay the same. This is why choosing people who already have X = x can differ from forcing X to equal x.

Mathematical background: Pearl, Causal inference in statistics (2009).

A worked example: adjusting for task difficulty

Suppose task difficulty Z affects both whether someone uses an assistant and whether they finish. Difficulty is a confounder: a common cause of X and Y. A direct comparison of users and non-users could mix up the assistant’s effect with differences in task difficulty.

For a valid backdoor adjustment set Z, we compare users and non-users within each difficulty group, then average using the same population weights:

P(Y = 1 | do(X = x))
= ∑z P(Y = 1 | X = x, Z = z) P(Z = z)

Here is an illustrative example, separate from the benchmark results. Assume half the tasks are easy and half are hard. Among assistant users, completion probabilities for easy and hard tasks are 0.90 and 0.60. Among non-users, they are 0.80 and 0.40.

With assistant: 0.90 × 0.50 + 0.60 × 0.50 = 0.75
Without assistant: 0.80 × 0.50 + 0.40 × 0.50 = 0.60

The average treatment effect (ATE) is the difference between these intervention outcomes. “Treatment” just means the action being tested; it need not be medical.

ATE = E[Y | do(X = 1)] − E[Y | do(X = 0)]
= 0.75 − 0.60 = 0.15E means “average.” For a yes/no outcome, its average is the probability of “yes.” Here, the intervention increases completion by 15 percentage points.

A standard sufficient condition for this adjustment is that the chosen Z blocks the noncausal paths entering X and contains no descendants of X, meaning variables caused by X directly or through other variables. Both assistant users and non-users must be represented within each difficulty group. Adjusting for arbitrary variables can introduce bias. A collider, for example, is a common effect of two variables; selecting or conditioning on it can create an association that was absent before.

How a counterfactual is calculated

A counterfactual keeps the observed case in view while changing one action. We first use what happened to update our beliefs about background factors, then change X, then calculate the alternative outcome.

  1. Abduction — learn from the observation. Update the distribution of background conditions from P(U) to P(U | E = e), where E = e is the evidence we observed.
  2. Action — change the decision. Replace X’s rule with X := x. Call this modified model 𝓜x.
  3. Prediction — work out the alternative. Evaluate Y in the changed model using those updated background conditions.
P(Yx = y | E = e)
= ∑u 𝟙[Y𝓜x(u) = y] P(U = u | E = e)For discrete U, add up the probabilities of background conditions in which the changed model produces y. The indicator 𝟙 is 1 when the condition is true and 0 otherwise. For continuous U, the sum becomes an integral.

Not every counterfactual can be answered from a graph and observed probabilities alone. The information and assumptions must determine a unique answer; this is called identification. CLadder constructs answerable problems. That does not mean an LLM is given every hidden variable or the generator’s full model.

Counterfactual framework: Pearl, Causal Inference (2010). Benchmark construction: CLadder paper, Sections 2–3.

Task formulation: what goes in, what comes out?

For item i, let qi be the full text question, ai its reference answer, and ei its reference explanation. The language model receives qi and predicts âi.

𝒟 = {(qi, ai, ei)}i = 1N
âi = fθ(qi),   ai, âi ∈ {yes, no}N is the number of evaluated questions. fθ is the model under its specified prompt and inference settings.

The official input fields are background, given_info, and question. The reference answer, reasoning, and ground-truth metadata must stay out of the model’s input.

CausalCoT is a prompting method that breaks the solution into six steps: extract the graph; identify the question type; write the symbolic question; collect the supplied probabilities; derive the formula to evaluate; and calculate the answer. That formula is called the estimand. Asking for these steps does not guarantee that the model follows them correctly.

How accuracy is measured

Accuracy is the fraction of questions answered correctly. For example, 90 correct answers out of 100 give 90% accuracy.

Accuracy (%)=100N∑i=1N𝟙[a^i=ai]For a rung or question type, calculate the same fraction using only the questions in that group. Overall accuracy is weighted by item counts, not necessarily the simple average of the three rung scores.

The balanced v1.5 benchmark contains 10,112 questions: 3,160 association, 3,160 intervention, and 3,792 counterfactual questions. Each rung has equal numbers of “yes” and “no” reference answers, giving a 50% uniform-random-guess baseline. For an actual run, the number of answers counted and any excluded questions still need to be recorded separately.

Task, prompting and dataset details: CLadder paper and official code and dataset documentation. The repository calls the balanced file cladder-v1-balanced.json.

Where did answers go wrong?

A separate trace analysis in the recorded results marks three kinds of mistake. An answer flip is a wrong final answer after apparently correct reasoning. A direction error reverses whether an effect increases or decreases an outcome. Backdoor-adjustment confusion mixes up valid confounder adjustment with harmful conditioning, such as conditioning on a collider.

For answer flips, the recorded analysis reports 115 of 892 wrong answers (12.9%) for Sonnet and 265 of 1,456 (18.2%) for Gemini. These error totals do not reconcile with the main leaderboard’s stated sample size and accuracy, so they are presented as a separately reported analysis. The category definitions and overlap also need confirmation from the annotation records.

Does forcing extra thinking help the same model?

The recorded follow-up reports that Claude Opus 4.8 did not return extended thinking on 4,608 of 10,105 items when it was optional. Rerunning that subset with a minimum budget of 1,024 thinking tokens increased reported accuracy from 88.60% to 90.23%, a gain of 1.63 percentage points.

There were 114 wrong-to-correct changes and 39 correct-to-wrong changes: roughly 2.9 corrections for each regression. Extra thinking helped on balance in this reported subset, but did not help every item or every question type.

05 · What remains unanswered

Open questions

The results show where to look next. The questions below are proposed follow-up tests, not findings already established by these runs.

  1. Does the answer survive a change in wording?

    If we rename variables or rewrite a question without changing its mathematics, does the model still give the same answer? That would help distinguish use of the causal structure from reliance on familiar phrasing.

    Test: make matched original and rewritten questions, keep the correct answer fixed, and compare each model’s answers item by item.

  2. Which step causes the mistake?

    A wrong answer can come from misunderstanding the graph, choosing the wrong formula, making an arithmetic mistake, or reversing the final yes/no label. Those failures need different fixes.

    Test: score the six CausalCoT steps separately against the reference solution, using a consistent annotation guide.

  3. When does extra thinking actually help?

    The Opus follow-up suggests an improvement on one subset, alongside some regressions. Does the gain remain when reasoning budgets are compared across all question types under otherwise matched settings?

    Test: use the same model, same questions, and fixed decoding settings; vary only the thinking budget and repeat the runs.

  4. Can models keep observation, intervention, and counterfactuals separate?

    The collider-bias scores suggest a useful place to probe conditioning mistakes. For counterfactuals, can models correctly infer background conditions from what happened before predicting what would have happened otherwise?

    Test: build matched cases that isolate collider conditioning, intervention, and counterfactual abduction, with intermediate answers that can be checked.

  5. Do these skills transfer beyond a fully specified puzzle?

    Real problems may have missing variables, uncertain causal relationships, noisy data, or too little information to identify an effect. A model also needs to recognise when the available evidence cannot support an answer.

    Test: gradually remove information or introduce uncertainty, and evaluate both answer correctness and appropriate statements of what cannot be concluded.
